## The core finding: the drafts are too well written

Victor rewrites almost every draft paragraph, and the direction of every rewrite is the
same: **from clever to ordinary.** The drafts read like a competent essayist - every
sentence compressed, balanced, quotable. The published voice reads like a sharp mate
talking - loose, plain, leaning on the same idioms everyone actually says.

The single most instructive edit on record: the draft wrote "You just can't untell
them." Victor shipped "you can't take it back." Same fact, coined phrase replaced by
the phrase a person would say. That substitution IS the house edit, repeated across
every edition.

The 17-Aug tell catalogue catches scaffolding (announced transitions, stock filler,
throat-clearing). It does not catch this, because none of these sentences is
scaffolding - each one is a deliberately engineered line. The register is the tell.

## What the drafts do that published never keeps ("phrases we DON'T use")

All examples below are verbatim from real drafts in the bank. None of them survived to
print, and the published counterparts contain nothing shaped like them.

1. **Mic-drop paragraph closers.** Every draft paragraph ends on a quotable reversal
   or wry compressed line. Published paragraphs end on a plain statement or a piece of
   advice.
   - "That's a favour rather than a policy, and it leaves when they do."
   - "Keeping her on side has been priced as something you pay for, and that's the bill."
   - "It's a humiliating amount of admin for something an adult already said yes to."
   - "...this is the best behaviour they're capable of."
   - "The mistakes are the only part guaranteed a close read."
   - "...exactly like a company where nobody needed to."
   Published paragraph endings, for contrast: "None of it is personal." / "Sometimes
   the team simply needs another person." / "Both of those are true at the same time."
   / "It stops being a parenting issue."

2. **Balanced antitheses / hinge sentences** (beyond the one-per-piece the catalogue
   already allows - the drafts average several per piece):
   - "A role that gets paused or pulled is a decision about the role. An offer that
     comes back lower... is about you."
   - "Informality coming down the org chart is warmth. Going the other way it's a
     development area."
   - "...you're not a candidate who walked away, you're a known flight risk at the
     same desk."
   - "...more information about you than about her."

3. **Coined phrases and clever compressions** where an ordinary idiom exists:
   - "can't untell them" (shipped as "can't take it back")
   - "which manager you drew"
   - "quietly reclassed as annual leave"
   - "spent their own capital on you... wore it on their own numbers"
   - "the year you went a bit funny about your name"

4. **The "more often than it should" family** - wry-understatement tics that appear in
   multiple drafts and zero published pieces: "works more often than it should", "beats
   the direct version more often than it should", "has a way of finding themselves",
   "tends to be obvious by the second week".

5. **"quietly [verb]"** as an atmosphere word: "quietly reclassed", "go quietly
   boring", "quietly regret". Published uses "quietly" almost never.

6. **Uniform compression.** In the drafts every sentence is maximally
   information-dense and none could be cut. Published pieces breathe: they repeat a
   beat ("Every team is different and workplace culture even more so"), open sentences
   with plain connectives, and include sentences that just carry the reader to the
   next point.

## What published actually does ("the exact writing specs")

**Shape** (unchanged from the 17-18 Aug refresh, still correct): 4 paragraphs
(occasionally 5), 280-340 words, 365 hard ceiling. Headline is a flat declarative with
a full stop or the literal campaign question. One screenshot sits between paragraphs.
Opener states the situation or mechanism plainly in sentence one.

**Register** (the missing half, now binding):

1. **Ordinary idiom over fresh phrasing, always.** The house voice runs on the
   expressions people actually say, including ones an AI would call cliches. Verbatim
   from published editions: "dodged a bullet", "red flag", "a carrot dangled in front
   of you", "living rent-free in your head", "gives you the ick", "throw the cat
   amongst the pigeons", "double edged sword", "smoke and mirrors", "suck it up",
   "read the room", "for many unfortunate souls". A tired idiom in the right place is
   the voice; a fresh coinage in its place is the tell.

2. **Aphorism budget: one per piece, maximum.** One genuinely sharp closing turn is
   allowed (13 Jul: "The fallout just reappears as tanking productivity, resentful
   exhausted parents, and a fractured workplace culture."). Everything else ends plain.

3. **Plain connective openers are wanted, not scaffolding.** Published sentences open
   with "In many cases,", "Same goes if...", "Of course,", "The catch is that...",
   "It's also...", "And the sad reality is that...", "Most people think... But in
   reality...", "This all goes without saying that...". (Distinct from the banned
   stock filler like "here's the thing" / "at the end of the day" - those are essay
   filler, these are speech.)

4. **Direct second-person advice is normal**, mid-piece and at the close: "you should
   never resign from your current role for a verbal offer", "Think about how much you
   trust your workplace... before deciding how much you want them to know", "keep in
   mind how it might come off".

5. **The brand has opinions**: "We reckon it sits in the same bucket as...", "we know
   its because you don't have much of a choice". ("We" still never sources claims to
   the DMs - the cardinal rule stands.)

6. **Inner-monologue questions ship** (3 Aug: "Was it condescending, endearing, or is
   that just how they talk? Am I reading too much into it?" and "But why is it usually
   junior staff being called those names...?"). These voice the reader's own thought.
   Essayist rhetorical questions ("The real question is whether...") remain banned.
   NOTE: this narrows the blanket "no rhetorical questions" house rule against the
   published evidence - flagged to Victor 24 Aug 2026.

7. **Looseness ships.** Comma splices, mild redundancy, two sentences in a row opening
   with "But", hedges ("probably", "usually", "sort of", "pretty standing") - all in
   print. Do not emulate typos, but do not polish to symmetry either. If every
   sentence in a paragraph could be a pull quote, the paragraph is wrong.

8. **No em dashes anywhere** (house rule, unchanged). Commas and full stops.
